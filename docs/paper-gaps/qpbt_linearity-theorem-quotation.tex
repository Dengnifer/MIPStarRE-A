\section{Key theorem forms}

Let $u\mapsto O^u$ be observables indexed by $\mathbb F_2^t$, acting on one
factor of a bipartite state $\rho$.  The cited theorem assumes the averaged
correlation estimate
\[
  \mathop{\mathbb E}_{u,v}
  \operatorname{Tr}_{\rho}\!\left(O^u O^v O^{u+v}\right) \geq 1-\delta.
\]
It produces an extension $\rho'$ and observables $L^u$ satisfying
\[
  L^uL^v=L^{u+v}
  \quad\text{for every }u,v,
  \qquad
  \mathop{\mathbb E}_{u}
  \lVert L^u-O^u\rVert_{\rho'}^2\leq\delta.
\]
In particular, $L^0=I$: exact linearity gives $(L^0)^2=L^0$, while an
observable is invertible.

The chapter needs a parameterized version.  For each pair $(x,z)$ it applies
the theorem to $(r,s)\mapsto O^{x,z,r,s}$.  The available hypothesis and the
required conclusion are both averaged over $(x,z)$.  Thus one must first
separate good and bad fibers, or formulate and prove the corresponding finite
averaged theorem directly.

\section{Comparison with the quoted statement}

Lines~711--725 of
\path{references/qpbt-paper/14_analysis_of_the_pauli_basis_test.tex} quote the
linearity theorem.  The displayed hypothesis correctly averages over both
group variables.  The displayed conclusion, however, says that the corrected
observable is close for every $u$, whereas Theorem~10 of the cited work gives
closeness only on average over $u$ \cite{Natarajan2017Linearity}.  The extra
assumption $\delta\leq1$ is harmless, and the assertion at the identity follows
from exact linearity as above.

Lines~825--832 construct the family $O^{x,z,r,s}$ and establish approximate
linearity only after averaging over $x,z,r,r',s,s'$.  They then apply the
single-family theorem separately for every $(x,z)$ and state that sufficiently
many zero ancillas avoid a further extension.  This leaves two steps implicit:
the global average does not provide the same error bound on every fiber, and
the theorem may return different extension spaces for different fibers.

The finite setting admits a standard repair.  Apply the theorem on fibers with
their actual defects, use Markov's inequality if a uniform good-fiber bound is
needed, and choose one finite auxiliary space large enough for every returned
space.  For each fiber, embed its auxiliary space so that its returned unit
vector maps to one fixed vector, and extend the conjugated observables by the
identity on the orthogonal complement.  The embeddings may depend on the fiber
because the observables do.  Averaging then yields the conclusion used later in
the chapter.  This is an open reduction, not a counterexample to the quoted
theorem or to the source's eventual combined-measurement conclusion.

\section{Conclusion}

The primary discrepancy is a quantifier mismatch in the quotation: the cited
linearity theorem gives average, not pointwise, closeness.  Its use for the
family indexed by $(x,z)$ also requires an explicit finite common-extension
argument.  Both are open formal reductions compatible with the intended
source conclusion.
